The biggest shift today isn’t which AI is smarter—it’s who controls access to services, transactions, and capital.

Alibaba’s Qwen now connects to phones, computers, and AI glasses. Users can ask a question, get recommendations, and even place an order instantly. AI competition is evolving from “answering questions” to “delivering services and completing transactions.”

Meanwhile, Nvidia is being called the “bank” of AI. It doesn’t just sell chips—it also provides capital and resources to companies across its ecosystem. Intel is raising $20 billion through a new share offering to invest in AI. The AI race has become a full-scale battle over access, computing power, and capital.

But as companies deploy AI agents in the real world, security governance must keep pace. When Datadog rolled out coding agents to more than 4,000 engineers, it used temporary credentials, least-privilege access, and pre-deployment reviews. What will determine whether AI can enter core business operations isn’t just model capability—it’s who controls access, who provides the capital, and who can keep the risks under control.
