Lower hardware requirements, combined with a permissive license, make private deployments more accessible to small and midsized teams. And the enormous download numbers and derivative ecosystem will continue to deepen developer loyalty.

A: Another model update from China comes from Zhipu AI. GLM-5.3 retains the same base architecture but scales up post-training. Internal evaluations show a 50% improvement in coding capabilities over version 5.2, and the company plans to release the model weights two weeks after launch.

B: This shows that model upgrades don’t always require retraining the base model. High-quality post-training data, environmental feedback, and tool-use optimization are emerging as a more efficient path to competitiveness. In the future, a large part of a model company’s engineering strength may come down to how well it trains models to use tools and complete real-world tasks.

A: But as model capabilities advance more quickly, the pressure on safety governance is becoming increasingly apparent. Anthropic disclosed that its internal Model 2 is slightly more capable than the publicly available Mythos 5 and is already being used for coding, agentic tasks, and data generation. At the same time, a biosafety classifier had gone nearly a year without running, affecting 133 million interactions.

B: This exposes a real problem: having comprehensive safety policies on paper doesn’t mean the controls are always working. Model 2 has performed well in some research and development tests, but it still trails experienced researchers. The real issue is that internal models are playing an increasingly prominent role in developing the next generation of models, so monitoring and review mechanisms need to keep pace.

A: The other side of Anthropic’s story is commercial growth. Bloomberg reports that the company’s second-quarter revenue rose more than fourteenfold year over year, providing a new valuation benchmark for a potential IPO. Meanwhile, as OpenAI moves ahead with preparations to go public, concerns over internal governance and organizational stability are heating up again.

B: Looking at the two companies together is revealing: enterprise demand for AI is rapidly translating into revenue, but going public puts governance structures, risk controls, and financial performance under the spotlight all at once. The faster a company grows, the more important it becomes to know whether the organization can withstand sustained scrutiny from the capital markets.

A: Beyond models, a new bottleneck is emerging in AI infrastructure. The chairman of SK Hynix expects next year to bring the most severe memory shortage in recent years. AI servers are driving up demand for HBM, and tech giants are racing to secure long-term supply agreements.

B: This means pressure on computing costs is spreading from processors to high-bandwidth memory. Major customers can lock in capacity ahead of time, while smaller customers may face higher prices and longer delivery times. When tracking AI investment from here on out, we can’t just watch GPUs—we also need to watch the memory supply chain.

B: Taken together, these two stories offer a very direct warning about risk: capital can quickly return to highly volatile instruments, but even seasoned institutions can suffer enormous losses in extreme market conditions. A rebound doesn’t mean the risks have disappeared.

Attacks on vessels in the Strait of Hormuz are increasing, and the United States has said it will intensify economic pressure on Iran, driving up risk premiums for both shipping security and energy. On the Russia-Ukraine battlefield, some of Ukraine’s Patriot launchers have been unable to continue intercepting targets after running out of ammunition.

B: Together, these developments show how supply constraints are reshaping the security landscape: on one side, critical shipping lanes and energy transportation; on the other, air-defense interceptor missiles. In both markets and warfare, inventories, production capacity, and resupply cycles are becoming decisive variables.
