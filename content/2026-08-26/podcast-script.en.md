A: Good morning, and welcome to today’s Personal Daily Brief.

B: The main story to watch today is that the AI race is expanding beyond “who has the most powerful model” to encompass chips, workplace gateways, data center financing, and even local politics and the global allocation of capital.

A: In other words, AI is no longer just a technology race. It has become a system-wide competition centered on computing power, capital, and real-world applications. Let’s start with chips at the very top of the supply chain.

B: At Hot Chips 2026, Arm unveiled its first internally designed, mass-produced AGI CPU in the company’s 36-year history. Built on TSMC’s N3P process with a dual-chiplet design, it features up to 136 cores, 100 billion transistors, and power consumption of 300 watts.

A: What really matters isn’t the specifications themselves, but the fact that Arm is moving beyond licensing architectures into finished chips. This could reshape its relationships with customers, partners, and even potential competitors. Competition in cloud-based AI agents will also increasingly depend on memory capacity, latency, and overall system integration efficiency.

B: When it comes to chips, Nvidia is impossible to ignore. The market is awaiting its latest earnings report for clues about how much genuine demand is behind AI capital spending.

A: But Nvidia now plays another role as well. By investing across the supply chain, it supports companies throughout the ecosystem while reinforcing demand for its own chips—almost like the “banker” of the AI industry. This model can expand the ecosystem, but it also binds investment valuations, customer financing, and chip orders more tightly together. If industry expectations cool, risks could ripple through the entire chain.

B: That brings us to today’s second theme: building AI infrastructure is becoming increasingly expensive, and it is now competing with governments for long-term capital.

A: Data centers require enormous upfront investment, but returns take time. They also face changing electricity costs, construction expenses, and customer demand. The Financial Times has also been examining the associated financing risks. More concretely, voters in some parts of the United States have begun opposing data center expansion, showing that AI infrastructure is constrained not only by funding, but also by power consumption, land use, and local politics.

B: So the market’s next focus may not simply be “how much has been built,” but whether projects have stable cash flow and whether their financing structures can withstand fluctuations in interest rates and demand.

A: The third theme shifts to the application layer. ByteDance has officially launched Doubao Work, which can generate content, support collaborative editing, perform visual tasks across different software, and even let users control their computers remotely from their phones.

B: Its key advantage is that it can inherit enterprise knowledge, context, and permission structures from Feishu. This moves workplace AI beyond a chat box that answers questions and toward a desktop agent that actually performs tasks. But once it enters the enterprise environment, security audits, permission boundaries, and the reliability of cross-application operations will matter far more than impressive demos.

A: Two distinct trends are also emerging in the model market. Alibaba has released smaller, lower-cost Qwen models, showing that competition is shifting toward deployment costs. Meanwhile, MiniMax has reportedly achieved nearly fourfold sales growth while narrowing its losses.

B: This means Chinese AI models are beginning to face the test of commercialization: whether businesses are willing to keep paying, and whether revenue growth can cover training and inference costs. As for Z.ai’s Ox Alpha, currently available summaries provide no specifications or benchmark results, so for now it is better treated as something to watch rather than something to draw conclusions about.

A: AI is also entering the core workflows of traditional finance. Google Gemini is changing how Deutsche Bank assesses credit risk.

B: But the available information does not clarify whether it merely organizes materials and assists with analysis, or directly influences lending decisions. The regulatory implications of those two roles are completely different. For banks adopting generative AI, the ultimate competition will be about more than efficiency. It will also hinge on whether outcomes can be explained, whether data use is compliant, and whether human review genuinely works.

A: Finally, let’s look at the macroeconomic environment. U.S. economic growth has been reported at 1.5%, with consumer spending and investment figures revised upward. Meanwhile, European Central Bank Executive Board member Isabel Schnabel has said that the economy’s strength means interest rates need to rise further.

B: And easing oil prices do not necessarily mean inflation has been resolved. The global cost of capital may remain elevated. This is especially important for the AI industry: on one side, computing investment continues to expand; on the other, government borrowing and high interest rates are competing for the same capital.

A: If there’s one line to remember today, it’s this: the heart of AI competition is shifting from model capabilities alone to chip supply, the cost of capital, enterprise gateways, and infrastructure capacity.

B: Technological progress remains rapid, but the factors that determine the winners in the next stage may be these seemingly less “technical” variables. That’s all for today’s Personal Daily Brief. See you next time.
