The second half of AI won’t be won by whoever builds the biggest model—but by whoever can connect chips, capital, and real-world business into one seamless chain.

The clearest signal comes from Arm. Thirty-six years after its founding, it’s moving beyond selling architectures and building its own chips for the first time: TSMC’s 3-nanometer process, up to 136 cores, and 100 billion transistors. This means the cloud AI computing market may soon have a new heavyweight player.

At the same time, AI is starting to do real work. ByteDance has launched Doubao Work, which can operate across different apps while tapping into Feishu’s knowledge and permission systems. Google’s Gemini has also entered Deutsche Bank’s credit risk assessment process. AI’s gateway is shifting from the chatbox to enterprise workflows.

But pressure is also mounting behind the boom. Nvidia is selling chips while investing in its customers, tying chip demand, financing, and valuations even more closely together. Data center financing risks are rising, and AI investment is even beginning to compete with governments for long-term capital.

So today’s most important takeaway is this: AI competition is evolving from a model race into an all-out battle over chips, applications, and capital efficiency. What truly matters next isn’t how many new models get launched, but who can keep generating revenue—and withstand the enormous cost of computing power.
