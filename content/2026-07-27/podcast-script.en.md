A: Welcome to today’s Personal Daily Briefing. The biggest story to watch today is how capital, energy, and policy are coming together to reshape the global technology race. AI remains the core driver, but the pace of expansion is no longer determined by model capabilities alone. It increasingly depends on who can provide the power, financing, infrastructure, and regulatory headroom.

B: And this trend is spilling over into employment, interest rates, manufacturing, and industrial policy. We’ve picked out six key areas to discuss.

A: Let’s start with financing for computing capacity. Nvidia is reportedly in talks to provide around $250 billion in guarantees for OpenAI’s data center project, which also plans to use electricity controlled by the US government.

B: This suggests that computing expansion is entering an era of mega-scale financing. Chip companies may no longer just sell equipment—they may also help customers solve their funding needs. But when supplier financing, customer purchases, and equity investments become interconnected, the market needs to ask: Is the demand behind these orders supported by end-user cash flow, or is it being generated by a cycle of capital?

A: Similar questions surround Nvidia’s more than $750 billion in AI-related deals. Going forward, assessing the quality of its revenue will require looking beyond order volume to whether customers can consistently generate real returns.

B: The second area is employment. Some major companies in technology, transportation, and defense have resumed hiring after roughly a year of hiring freezes. That does not align with simplistic predictions that AI would quickly eliminate large numbers of jobs.

A: A more accurate approach may be to look not only at the number of jobs, but at how tasks are structured. OpenAI research also shows that ChatGPT is helping employees complete work that previously fell outside the scope of their roles.

B: So in the short term, the returns from corporate AI adoption are more likely to come from greater employee productivity and expanded capabilities. At the same time, training, job redesign, and collaborative workflows will become new areas of cost.

A: The third area is monetary policy. Inflationary pressure is rising again in the United States. This week, the Federal Reserve will not only discuss keeping interest rates unchanged—the market may even need to reconsider the small possibility of another rate hike.

B: Recent volatility in oil prices makes the outlook even more complicated. Higher oil prices can push up inflation expectations, but they can also weigh on growth. Bond yields have already risen, and markets will now closely watch the Fed’s language and any disagreements among officials.

A: The European Central Bank may also raise rates again in September, according to reports. Meanwhile, the Swiss National Bank may keep rates at zero through the end of 2027. Global monetary policy will not move in lockstep, which could lead to greater divergence in exchange rates and cross-border capital flows.

B: The fourth area is China’s industrial foundation. China is planning “six major networks” for the AI era, aiming to coordinate long-term infrastructure investment across computing capacity, electricity, data, and communications networks.

A: This is critical because chips are no longer the only bottleneck in the race between AI models. Electricity costs, the efficiency of data flows, and the regional availability of computing capacity will all affect the training costs of Chinese-developed models.

B: At the same time, although industrial profit growth slowed slightly in June, global AI demand continues to support related manufacturing and exports. Whether industrial policy can translate into stable profits will depend on the pace of project investment and actual utilization rates.

A: The fifth area is semiconductors. ChangXin Technology raised $8.55 billion through an IPO, with the funds earmarked for memory chip technology and domestic substitution. Capital is clearly concentrating in critical areas of the hardware supply chain.

B: Another report—that China has begun producing DUV lithography equipment—triggered a decline in ASML’s share price. However, the publicly available summary currently lacks key information, including equipment performance, production volume, and the manufacturers involved.

A: So what matters more here is the shift in market expectations, rather than prematurely concluding that the technological landscape has already changed. The next things to watch are mass-production capacity, yield rates, and supporting supply chains.

B: The sixth area brings us back to regulation and capital flows. AI companies’ lobbying spending in Washington has reached a record high, showing that competition has expanded beyond models and products into the drafting of regulatory rules.

A: From the United States to Japan, policymakers are also steering private capital toward priority industries. China, meanwhile, is tightening oversight of overseas investments and offshore structures used by the ultra-wealthy. Taken together, capital allocation is increasingly being shaped by national industrial priorities, tax rules, and security considerations.

B: Finally, let’s turn to Argentina. IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva has met with President Javier Milei as Argentina faces a debt bill of around $57 billion.

A: The key question is whether the IMF recognizes the results of Milei’s reforms and adjusts the repayment arrangements. This will affect not only foreign-exchange liquidity and Argentina’s sovereign risk premium, but also how much room Milei has to continue pursuing fiscal austerity.

B: To sum up today: AI expansion remains a major engine, but it is increasingly being shaped by financing costs, power supplies, employment structures, industrial policy, and regulatory rules.
