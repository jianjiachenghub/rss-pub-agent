The biggest shift today isn’t another new large model. It’s that AI competition is moving from “Who’s smarter?” to “Who can keep working more cheaply and reliably?”

Zhipu has just open-sourced GLM-5.3: 743 billion parameters, a million-token context window, and long-running agents officially entering the open-weight race. But its FP8 weights still take up 756 GB—meaning the barrier has simply shifted from model capability to compute, memory, and inference infrastructure.

Meanwhile, OpenAI has fixed abnormal usage metering in Codex and ChatGPT Work and reset users’ quotas. The same quota is now expected to get 10% to 50% more work done. At the same time, Codex is testing a “persistent mode,” which could let agents proactively execute tasks for extended periods.

This means companies can’t evaluate AI based on token prices alone. They need to ask whether a task can actually be completed reliably—and account for risks like runaway permissions, compounding errors, and model service disruptions.

a16z’s launch of its “Machine Age Fund” reinforces this view: the next wave of AI gains may not belong only to model companies, but also to chips, networking, memory, power, and cooling.

In one sentence: AI is moving beyond the model race and into a hard-fought battle over infrastructure and continuous delivery.
