Now, as AI bottlenecks spread across the entire computing stack, scarce hardware and long-term production capacity are becoming growth assets again. But these investments require longer time horizons and more capital, so their return profiles look very different from software projects.

A: On the product side, OpenAI has confirmed that it is testing a Codex “persistent mode” that would allow agents to work proactively for extended periods, though there are no plans for a broad rollout in the near term.

B: This suggests coding agents could move from “assign a task, complete a task” to continuous operation. But the longer they run, the greater the risks of permission misuse, runaway costs, and compounding errors. What companies really need to consider may not be how long an agent can work continuously, but when it should stop automatically and which steps must be reviewed by a human.

A: Meanwhile, OpenAI has fixed eight categories of abnormal usage and reset limits for paying Codex and ChatGPT Work users. The company says the same quota can now complete roughly 10% to 50% more work.

B: This reminds us that evaluating agent costs is not just about the price per token. Context compression, leftover image data, and goal-state management all affect consumption. A more meaningful metric would be: how much does it actually cost to complete one useful task?

A: Model supply relationships are shifting as well. OpenAI says it will stop supplying models to Cursor. After SpaceX acquired Cursor for $60 billion, Anthropic said it would increase compute support for Claude.

B: For developer tools, stable model supply is becoming a critical factor alongside model performance. Cursor may become more reliant on Claude, while OpenAI could lose an important gateway into coding workflows. Post-acquisition compliance reviews will also continue to shape the boundaries of cooperation between platforms and model providers.

A: Turning to the macro picture, after the Jackson Hole meeting, markets are refocusing on inflation, employment, and the Federal Reserve’s next steps. Warsh’s proposed forward-looking policy framework has also prompted investors to ask whether the future anchor for interest rates could change.

B: The most important near-term test will come from U.S. employment data. If the labor market cools significantly, it will become harder for the Fed to maintain a hawkish stance. If employment remains resilient, high interest rates may persist for longer. It’s also worth noting that rising bond yields have already added tens of billions to the debt-servicing costs of G7 countries, with interest payments gradually squeezing their fiscal room.

A: Capital allocation is changing too. As investors look to reduce their concentrated exposure to the AI theme, pharmaceutical stocks are attracting more interest.

B: That does not necessarily mean the market is abandoning AI. It looks more like a rebalancing away from an overcrowded trade, with some capital shifting toward pharmaceuticals, where earnings are relatively stable and demand is more defensive.

Its product mix, channel expenses, and pricing structure may also be factors. Going forward, we need to watch inventory reduction and end-market sales rather than focusing only on the figures from a single quarterly report.

A: To sum up today, as AI moves toward longer-running tasks and heavier infrastructure, macro markets are also recalculating the long-term cost of high interest rates.
