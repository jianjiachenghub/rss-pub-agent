A: Good morning, and welcome to today’s Personal Daily Briefing. The biggest story to watch today is that the AI industry is entering a phase of “greater scale and lower costs—but returns that are harder to calculate.”

B: On one hand, investment in chips, data centers, and storage continues to grow. On the other, model prices keep falling, prompting the market to ask: after spending all this money, will companies ultimately earn it back? Let’s start with Nvidia.

A: Bloomberg reported on August 27 that Nvidia is in talks to acquire Hugging Face for $13 billion, though the deal has not yet been finalized. Hugging Face is not only a model-hosting platform, but also a major gateway into the open-source AI ecosystem.

B: If the deal goes through, Nvidia would gain more than just a software platform—it would gain access to developers, models, and distribution channels. At the same time, however, the United States is investigating chip shipments to China. In other words, Nvidia’s expansion plans are colliding with export-control risks, so the deal’s terms and the regulatory outlook still bear watching.

A: What supports Nvidia’s continued expansion is persistently strong demand for computing power. The company’s sales more than doubled year over year to $96 billion, beating Wall Street expectations, and it projects sales growth of around 70% in the next fiscal year.

B: That forecast also lifted Nasdaq futures, Asian equities, and AI-related stocks. But the market is no longer looking only at revenue growth—it is also asking how long customers can sustain their capital spending. After all, investment in chips keeps rising while AI token prices are falling, and the return on enterprise investment has yet to be fully proven.

A: The infrastructure race is also spreading into storage and financing. SK Hynix has broken ground on a $4 billion memory hub in Indiana, while Kioxia and SanDisk plan to invest more than $31 billion in Japan to expand flash-memory supply.

B: Model companies are also locking in computing capacity ahead of time. Anthropic has reached a $45 billion data-center partnership with British startup Nscale, while JPMorgan is leading efforts to raise $5 billion in debt to fund Volta AI’s expansion. What is worth noting is that AI infrastructure is relying less exclusively on companies’ own funds and increasingly on long-term contracts and debt financing—bringing an accumulation of financial risk along with it.

A: Meanwhile, Chinese model developers are pursuing competition from another direction: reducing the cost per unit of capability. Alibaba has open-sourced Qwen3.8-Flash, a 125-billion-parameter multimodal mixture-of-experts model that activates only 6 billion parameters per token and supports context windows of up to 1 million tokens.

B: Its training cost is reportedly only about one-ninth that of Qwen3.7-Plus. Zhipu’s open-source GLM-5.3-Flash uses a hybrid architecture combining sparse and linear attention to reduce attention computation and KV-cache usage, and is supported by 100,000 domestically produced accelerators. The focus of competition is shifting from “How large is the model?” to “How much does the same level of capability actually cost?”

A: Business models are changing as well. Moonshot AI is reportedly in talks with Microsoft, Amazon, and Google to host Kimi K3, seeking a share of up to 30% of service revenue.

B: If an agreement is reached, open-weight models would no longer have to rely solely on direct API sales—they could also benefit from distribution revenue through cloud platforms. That 30% figure could even become a new benchmark in negotiations between model companies and cloud distributors. For now, however, these are still only reported talks, and a formal agreement remains to be seen.

A: Turning to the macroeconomic picture, Federal Reserve official Beth Hammack said current interest rates have not yet reached a restrictive level and that action should be taken now. European Central Bank officials are also discussing maintaining a “moderately restrictive” policy stance and may return to a data-dependent approach after raising rates in September.

B: This suggests that interest rates in the United States and Europe may remain high for longer. Signs of stress have already emerged in private credit markets: some investors would rather keep their money locked up than exit at a loss of around 26%. A lack of transactions does not necessarily mean prices are stable—sometimes it simply means the losses have not yet been recognized.

A: Finally, let’s look at two issues that can easily be overshadowed by headlines about computing power. Google has moved its AI responsibility team out of DeepMind, raising employee concerns about its ability to conduct independent reviews. Meanwhile, a UK survey found that nearly half of young people mistakenly believe AI-generated financial advice is already protected by regulation.

B: One issue concerns internal checks and balances within companies; the other concerns users’ understanding of the boundaries of AI services. The faster technology is deployed, the more important it becomes to establish clear institutional arrangements defining whether responsibility teams can independently challenge product goals—and who regulates financial advice.

A: Today’s key themes can be summed up in four words: access, computing power, cost, and returns.

B: Investment in the AI industry is still accelerating, but what truly determines the winners in the next phase may not be who spends the most. It may be who can turn computing power into recurring revenue while keeping financing, regulatory, and governance risks under control.
