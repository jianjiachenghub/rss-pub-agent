B: Exactly. Models continue to improve and user numbers continue to rise, but expanding computing capacity requires more electricity and financing. It is also beginning to affect corporate finances, local resources, cybersecurity, and even international trade. Let’s look at today’s key developments through that lens.

A: Let’s start with open-source models. Alibaba’s Qwen team has open-sourced Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, with 2.4 trillion total parameters and 95 billion active parameters. It is also Qwen’s most powerful open-source model to date.

B: What makes this significant is that Max-level capabilities, which were previously usually reserved for closed-source products, are now entering the open-source ecosystem. The real questions to watch next aren’t just about benchmark scores, but whether the community can deploy it at a reasonable cost and whether companies will use it to develop industry-specific models and agents.

A: Model commercialization is also accelerating. DeepSeek V4 Pro has officially launched across the app, website, and API. At the same time, reports say DeepSeek has raised the price of its flagship model as it prepares for an IPO.

B: This shows that the industry is beginning to test a very practical question: can model capabilities translate into pricing power? Meanwhile, Google has announced that Gemini now has more than one billion monthly active users. The measure of competition is shifting from model leaderboards to user scale, retention, and revenue.

A: But the cost of that growth is showing up first in computing infrastructure. Tencent reported operating cash flow of 52.7 billion yuan in the second quarter, while free cash flow fell to negative 13.8 billion yuan. Excluding prepayments for computing capacity, however, it would have been positive 37.6 billion yuan.

B: So this looks more like cash spending being brought forward by the timing of purchases than a sudden deterioration in the business. What investors need to watch next is how quickly those prepayments turn into usable computing capacity—and whether that capacity can then generate revenue from advertising, gaming, and cloud services.

A: Capital markets are also continuing to finance this wave of expansion. AMD plans to raise $4.75 billion through a bond offering, while Wall Street is eagerly underwriting bond and stock issuances from AI companies. Meanwhile, Anthropic is reportedly discussing the acquisition of Decart for around $6 billion.

B: The concern is that the massive flow of capital into AI could squeeze financing opportunities for other companies. Another report today noted that credit risk is being repriced even for financially sound companies with no connection to AI. An AI boom does not mean every asset will benefit equally.

A: An even harder constraint than funding is electricity. Texas is becoming less welcoming toward AI data centers, while India’s power grid is simultaneously facing rising electricity demand, climate change, and additional loads from new data centers.

B: This means data center locations can’t be chosen based only on land and tax incentives. Grid capacity—and whether local residents are willing to bear the costs—also matter. Microsoft’s decision to cut its carbon-removal purchases by 80% is another reminder that as computing investment rises rapidly, companies’ earlier climate commitments may find themselves competing for budget.

A: On security and governance, Taiwan has reportedly suffered a highly autonomous AI-powered cyberattack, though the full scope has not yet been disclosed. U.S. trade enforcement officials have also begun using AI to identify attempts to evade tariffs.

B: The same capabilities are entering both attack and enforcement processes at once. Companies need to guard against more than faster automated attacks; they also need to address the lack of transparency in model-driven decisions. Another study suggests that leading models may exhibit systematic bias on sensitive issues involving China. This shows that when procuring models, companies shouldn’t test only for accuracy—they also need to examine moderation boundaries, regional differences, and the sources behind the answers.

A: Finally, let’s look at the broader markets. The U.S. inflation outlook has led traders to scale back bets on further interest-rate hikes, but Federal Reserve official Beth Hammack still questions whether inflation can continue to slow. In Japan, there are reports of support for the central bank to accelerate rate hikes in order to strengthen the yen.

B: Risk appetite in emerging markets is also cooling. Foreign investors are pulling out of Brazilian stocks at the fastest pace since 2021. Meanwhile, as retail enthusiasm and liquidity weaken, the crypto market is entering a phase of deeper institutional participation.

A: To sum up today: model capabilities and user numbers are still growing, but the factors that may determine the winners in the next stage are financing costs, electricity supply, cash-flow conversion, and governance capabilities.
