A: Good morning, and welcome to today’s Personal Daily Briefing.

B: The biggest story today is that AI competition is shifting from “who has the best model” to “who can shoulder the enormous infrastructure and financing costs.” Chips, electricity, data centers, credit guarantees, and even payments and settlement are all becoming part of the same value chain.

A: Let’s start with Nvidia and OpenAI. Reports say Nvidia plans to invest or provide roughly $100 billion in support for a data center project in Ohio. The campus will be used by OpenAI and is designed to consume up to 10 gigawatts of power.

B: What’s easy to overlook is that Nvidia isn’t just selling GPUs. It may also invest in energy companies, provide credit guarantees, and lock in OpenAI’s demand through long-term leases. In other words, chipmakers are moving into power, real estate, and financing all at once. But this kind of deep integration could also transfer the customer’s credit risk back to Nvidia.

A: The true cost of AI infrastructure may also be much higher than the publicly disclosed figures. According to reports, off-balance-sheet obligations taken on by tech giants through leases, guarantees, and similar arrangements could put AI-related spending as much as $3 trillion above reported levels.

B: That means we can’t assess AI investment by looking at capital expenditure alone. We also need to consider future payment commitments. The insurance shortfall surrounding Meta and Blackstone’s $14 billion data center project is a warning: when projects scale too quickly, lending, insurance, and underwriting capacity may not be able to keep up.

A: Another important gateway to watch is OpenRouter. Stripe is reportedly acquiring the model aggregation platform for more than $7 billion. OpenRouter connects over 400 models and serves more than 8 million developers.

B: What Stripe sees probably goes beyond model usage fees. It’s interested in the entire gateway for deciding which model to call, who pays, and how agents settle transactions automatically. Stripe is also using coding agents internally to increase product output, rather than simply cutting staff. In the future, payments may gradually shift from website checkouts to machine identities, authorized spending limits, and automated transactions.

A: Now let’s turn to Asia. Alibaba, Baidu, and Kuaishou are facing rising costs for model development, computing power, and operations. China’s domestic AI race is also entering a phase where profits and cash flow will be put to the test.

B: At the same time, Unitree Robotics will list on the STAR Market on August 19. Humanoid robotics finally has a more direct public-market pricing benchmark. After the listing, investors will shift their focus from impressive demonstrations to production scale, gross margins, order quality, and whether R&D investment can be sustained.

A: Japan is the main focus in macro markets today. Slower economic growth is making it harder for the Bank of Japan to determine the timing and pace of its next rate hike. But markets are still betting on tighter policy, and Japan’s 10-year government bond yield has risen to its highest level since 1996.

B: This creates a contradiction: the economic data calls for caution from the central bank, while the bond market is already pushing up long-term borrowing costs. In the United States, Goldman Sachs believes the market’s pricing of Federal Reserve rate hikes is too hawkish. As rate-hike expectations cool, emerging-market currencies have climbed to record highs, but investors have become noticeably more selective about inflation-linked bonds across different countries.

A: Risk assets are also diverging. European Central Bank economists have warned that U.S. tech stocks could pull back. And after Bitcoin’s price stalled, Bitcoin ETFs saw their largest outflow in six weeks.

B: Meanwhile, London copper prices are approaching record highs, with tight spot supply and rising prices reinforcing each other. The common signal is that capital is not simply fleeing risk assets. Instead, investors are reassessing which high valuations are supported by cash flow and which price increases are driven by genuine supply and demand.

A: Finally, let’s look at two developments outside the tech sector. Tensions in the Middle East continue to rise. Reports say the United States has expanded its military pressure to include Oman, which has acted as a mediator. Other intelligence suggests that Iranian hardliners are adjusting their strategy toward the United States and its allies. These reports still need ongoing verification, but the risk of miscalculation is clearly increasing.

B: In pharmaceuticals, WuXi AppTec has attracted strong market interest because of its position as a key supplier in the GLP-1 value chain. This reflects another kind of “infrastructure value”: when certain production capacity cannot be replaced quickly, geopolitical constraints and industrial dependence both become part of the valuation.

A: To sum up today, AI remains the central theme. But the next stage may be decided not by model parameters, but by who controls electricity, financing, payments, and large-scale delivery.

B: At the same time, Japanese interest rates, risks in the Middle East, copper supply and demand, and critical pharmaceutical production capacity are reminding us that today’s market is not just a single AI-driven rally. Multiple capital and industrial value chains are being repriced at once. That’s all for today’s Personal Daily Briefing. See you next time.
