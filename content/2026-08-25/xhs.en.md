Title: Today’s Must-Reads: Chips, Markets, and Geopolitical Risks

August 25 brought a flood of information. What truly deserves attention is not just the latest AI product launches, but the simultaneous shifts taking place across the computing-power supply chain, global capital flows, and geopolitical risks.

① The computing-power race enters the “in-house chip” stage

OpenAI says its new custom chip outperformed Nvidia processors in testing. Since the model and test conditions have not yet been disclosed, it is too early to determine which is stronger. But the signal is clear: leading AI companies are moving from “scrambling to buy chips” to “building their own.”

Xiaomi also unveiled the XRING O100, an on-device AI accelerator focused on addressing memory-bandwidth constraints and inference costs when running large models on smartphones. More AI tasks may eventually be completed directly on phones, but power consumption, heat dissipation, and mass-production capacity will be the real tests.

② The AI boom is not just for chip companies

Taiwan’s King Slide Works has benefited from growth in its server-rack rail business, making founder Lin Tsung-chi Taiwan’s richest person. The case shows that opportunities created by data-center expansion are spilling over into essential components such as rails, cooling systems, power supplies, and connectors.

Meanwhile, an Indian AI data-center company has placed an advance order for 9,000 Nvidia Vera Rubin chips. The global buildout of computing capacity continues, but competition now extends to securing production capacity and supporting supply chains.

③ Tech giants begin competing for the “gateway to work”

ByteDance has consolidated its workplace AI offerings under the “Doubao Work” brand and integrated the TRAE and Coze teams, pushing the competition directly into enterprise productivity and AI-agent use cases.

But as workplace AI becomes more deeply embedded, data boundaries become increasingly sensitive. Some companies want AI to read employees’ Slack messages for search, summarization, and task execution. Efficiency may improve, but organizations must first clarify who can access internal communications, how the data will be used, and who will be accountable when something goes wrong.

④ Medical AI gains more solid clinical evidence

DAMO LiON, jointly developed by DAMO Academy and hospitals, identified 15 previously missed malignant tumors in CT scans during a prospective trial. Most lesions were about one centimeter in size.

The key this time is not simply “higher accuracy,” but that AI is beginning to be validated in real clinical workflows. Large-scale adoption will still require multicenter trials, false-positive assessments, and medical-device approval.

⑤ Markets are taking risks and retreating at the same time

Bitcoin has broken above $80,000, while access through Wall Street has become cheaper and more convenient, continuing to lower the barrier for institutions to allocate capital to crypto assets.

At the same time, Kraken was hit by a “dusting attack,” with numerous tiny transfers used to disrupt address tracking. This shows that as crypto assets grow in scale, regulation and anti-money-laundering enforcement become more difficult.

Stock markets are also showing signs of cooling: South Korean leveraged ETFs saw roughly $1 billion in outflows, while the country’s stock market suffered a sharp pullback after the AI-fueled rally. Once a popular trade becomes overcrowded, reversals often happen faster than expected.

⑥ New shifts emerge in macro capital flows

The Bank of Japan’s preferred inflation indicator remains above 2%, leaving room for further rate hikes. Meanwhile, U.S. consumer confidence has fallen to a seven-month low, reflecting greater household caution about the economic outlook.

HSBC’s purchase of at least $3 billion in Indian bonds also suggests that some international investors are seeking new fixed-income opportunities.

⑦ Geopolitical conflict continues to shape energy and technology

Ukraine’s two-pronged drone attacks disrupted Russian oil supplies, renewing concerns over energy transportation risks. The U.K. is using real-world combat data from Ukraine to train military AI, making battlefield data a scarce resource in the competition over defense algorithms.

Taiwan’s prosecution of nine people suspected of reselling AI servers to mainland China also signals that restrictions on advanced computing power are expanding beyond chip exports to server resales, end-user verification, and supply-chain employee accountability.

Three key takeaways from today:

- AI competition is expanding from models into chips, workplace gateways, and real-world industry applications.
- Capital continues to chase computing power and crypto assets, but crowded trades are beginning to diverge sharply.
- Technology, capital, and geopolitics are becoming increasingly difficult to view separately.
