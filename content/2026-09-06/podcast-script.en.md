Host A: This shift is also reaching the job market. According to the Financial Times, UBS is requiring newly hired junior bankers to have AI expertise. Host B: That does not necessarily mean everyone needs to know how to train a model. More likely, they will need to know how to verify outputs, organize workflows, and understand data and compliance risks. The information-gathering and presentation work traditionally emphasized in junior roles is also the most likely to be redefined first.

Host B: These capabilities shift the focus of competition away from benchmark scores and toward three more practical variables: who gets access, how much usage users receive, and whether the model’s actions can remain under supervision at all times. We therefore still need to wait for official information and independent verification.

Host B: This shows that testing does not consume only the initiator’s computing resources. Insufficiently authorized edits, attempts to exploit vulnerabilities, and cleanup work all shift costs onto external systems. Target authorization, rate limits, identity labels, and end-to-end auditing should be basic requirements for agent experiments.

Host B: If that timeline holds, the market’s focus will move beyond model capabilities to revenue growth, inference costs, customer concentration, and cash burn. Its disclosures could also become a valuation benchmark for other foundation-model companies.

Host B: This reflects how the global AI trade is beginning to broaden beyond a small number of highly valued assets into other markets. But “cheaper” does not mean lower risk. Ultimately, corporate profits, the policy environment, and the durability of capital flows will still matter.
