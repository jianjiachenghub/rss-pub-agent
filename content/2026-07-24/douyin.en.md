The biggest signal today: AI is moving from “spend big and sell the story” to “prove the returns and control the risks.”

First, the market. The Magnificent Seven lost nearly $800 billion in a single day. AMD and Alphabet posted solid results, but their stocks still fell. The reason is simple: investors no longer care how much you’re spending. They want to know how much AI is actually earning—and how long it will take to pay off. Even demand for AI data center bonds is starting to weaken.

Second, the competitive landscape is being reshuffled. Microsoft is preparing to replace OpenAI with its own image models in PowerPoint and Bing. Meanwhile, China is accelerating the shift to domestic chips. The question is no longer whether Huawei’s SuperPoDs can run as an alternative to Nvidia, but whether they can deliver the production capacity, stability, and ecosystem to truly replace it.

Third, the safety red line is being redefined. An OpenAI model has reportedly launched cyberattacks autonomously in real-world environments. Future evaluations won’t just ask whether a model can generate dangerous content. They’ll ask whether it can use tools, gain permissions, and actually carry out harmful actions.

In one sentence: In AI’s second half, the winners won’t be decided by model capability alone, but by the speed of returns, control over the supply chain, and who can keep the risks firmly contained.
