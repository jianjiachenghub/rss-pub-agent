A: Good morning, and welcome to today’s Personal Daily Briefing. The key theme to watch today is the accelerating rollout of technology across industries, even as policy, supply-chain, and security constraints tighten at the same time.

B: That’s right. From the hurdles facing the launch of Apple Intelligence in China, to funding for domestic chipmakers, to an AI agent accidentally connecting to the open internet, the competition is no longer just about model capabilities. It is also about compliance, supply-chain resilience, and engineering controls. Meanwhile, interest rates, energy, and inflation continue to shape market pricing.

A: Let’s start with Apple Intelligence. Apple’s Simplified Chinese website briefly displayed instructions for using Alibaba’s Qwen on Mac, but the page was later removed. Customer service also said it had not received any official launch notice.

B: This detail suggests that the technical preparations may already be well advanced, but the product announcement, regulatory approval, and official release are not yet fully aligned. So this does not mean the feature will be available immediately. It is more of a reminder to the market that the rollout of Apple Intelligence in China still faces uncertainty over timing.

A: Apple’s other piece of news concerns its hardware supply chain. According to reports, the company is testing memory chips from CXMT and has held preliminary supply discussions, potentially for use first in selected iPhones and MacBooks sold in China.

B: The backdrop is tight global memory supply. If testing eventually leads to procurement, CXMT could gain entry into the qualification system of a top-tier consumer electronics company, while Apple could reduce purchasing costs and supply-disruption risks. The question to watch is whether this develops from a regional solution for the Chinese market into a long-term part of Apple’s supply-chain structure.

A: China’s AI industry, meanwhile, is seeing competition over both financing and product gateways. Moore Threads says it plans to list in Hong Kong at an appropriate time. Separately, Tencent is reportedly concentrating its distribution channels, computing capacity, and organizational resources on its AI workplace product, WorkBuddy.

B: These two developments represent capital and applications, respectively. AI chip companies need continuous financing to support R&D and capacity expansion, and Hong Kong could offer a new valuation window. Tencent, meanwhile, hopes to use the distribution power of WeChat and WeCom to turn AI productivity software from a standalone tool into a major gateway. In both cases, the real test is whether investment can be converted into stable revenue.

A: The public market is also about to deliver a pricing test for Unitree Robotics. The company opened subscriptions on August 10 at an issue price of 150.80 yuan per share. It plans to raise about 6.1 billion yuan, implying a market capitalization of nearly 61 billion yuan, and demand from institutional investors has been very strong.

B: This offering could become a new valuation benchmark for the humanoid robotics sector. The participation of the National Social Security Fund, industrial investors, and DeepSeek in the placement has also strengthened the market’s connection between robotics hardware and model capabilities. But strong subscription demand is one thing; delivering production capacity, orders, and profits after listing is another.

A: Now to AI development and safety. Zhang Yiming has reportedly opposed model distillation, hoping that Seed will reduce its dependence on external models and renew its focus on developing models from the ground up. Legal AI company Harvey, meanwhile, has open-sourced an evaluation project that uses standardized tasks to measure whether legal AI agents can actually do the job.

B: Both point to the same shift: the industry is beginning to value sustainable, real-world capabilities, not just impressive demos. But greater capability also brings new risks. A Meta model connected to the internet because of a misconfigured testing environment and gained access to systems belonging to other organizations. Before AI agents go live, network isolation, least-privilege access, and clear responsibility for third-party assessments are likely to become mandatory requirements.

A: Turning to global markets, U.S. investors are awaiting the latest inflation data to determine whether expectations for a September rate hike need to be adjusted. At the same time, Wall Street has interpreted recent remarks from Treasury Secretary Bessent as a sign that the government is becoming concerned about a further rise in long-term Treasury yields.

B: This means the market needs to watch both inflation and fiscal policy. If price pressures do not cool sufficiently, expectations for short-term rates may remain tight. If the government clearly signals that it wants to contain long-term bond yields, it could adjust debt maturities, fiscal messaging, and liquidity arrangements. Emerging Asian markets are also making greater use of swaps and liquidity tools to stabilize their currencies, reducing the need to draw directly on foreign-exchange reserves.

A: Finally, energy. Oman says negotiations concerning the Strait of Hormuz are progressing smoothly, but Iran says that even if an agreement is reached, shipping will not resume immediately.

B: So what really matters is not the language used in negotiations, but when vessels can actually begin passing through normally again. As long as the resumption of shipping continues to be delayed, the risk premiums embedded in oil prices, freight rates, and insurance costs will be difficult to unwind completely. Still, there are signs that the oil shock is easing. Growth in both Chinese industrial and consumer prices is slowing, giving policymakers more room to act. At the same time, it is important to watch whether weaker consumer prices reflect insufficient domestic demand.

A: European markets are currently more upbeat, with strong earnings temporarily outweighing war-related risks and capital flowing back into European equities.

B: There are three things to remember today: when Apple Intelligence will officially launch in China, when shipping through the Strait of Hormuz will actually resume, and whether U.S. inflation can alter the path of interest rates. That’s all for today’s Personal Daily Briefing. See you next time.
