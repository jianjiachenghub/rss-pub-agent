A: Good morning, and welcome to today’s Personal Daily Brief.

B: The biggest story today is how artificial intelligence is evolving from a technology race into a sweeping realignment of capital, industry, and regulation. Companies continue to invest, but the cost of capital, commercial returns, and legal risks are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.

A: Meanwhile, both oil prices and interest rates are rising, putting clear pressure on market risk appetite. Let’s begin with the leadership changes at Apple.

---

A: John Ternus has succeeded Tim Cook as Apple’s CEO, and the new leadership team will reportedly give artificial intelligence greater priority.

B: The key question isn’t whether Apple will pursue AI, but whether its product cadence will change. Apple has traditionally placed greater emphasis on maturity, privacy, and hardware-software integration. If the new leadership accelerates investment, people will be watching to see whether these capabilities make their way into phones, computers, and operating systems more quickly.

A: This also means Apple now has to answer two questions at once: whether its AI experience is differentiated enough, and whether heavy investment can translate into new revenue.

---

B: The flow of capital across the industry is also quite interesting. Stakes in other AI companies held by tech giants have already generated around $160 billion in gains. On the other hand, Huawei’s net profit fell 37% in the first half of the year, partly because of rising R&D and overall costs.

A: Taken together, these developments show that returns on AI investment are highly uneven. Owning shares in high-profile companies can quickly generate paper gains, while actually building models, chips, and infrastructure tends to squeeze profits first.

B: So when assessing corporate performance going forward, it won’t be enough to ask whether a company has an AI strategy. We also need to examine whether its returns come from operations, investment valuations, or long-term investments that have yet to pay off.

---

A: On the cost side, Barclays has provided some revealing figures: for every $100 a model company earns, roughly $35 to $40 goes to AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud, with the cloud providers potentially keeping $10 to $20 as profit.

B: This shows that even as model companies grow their revenue, their gross margins will remain constrained by inference costs and the bargaining power of cloud providers.

A: At the same time, traditional software companies are spending more to retain customers and fill gaps in their AI capabilities. The future of software competition will be about both products and costs: the companies that can deliver reliable services with lower inference expenses will be the ones most likely to protect their margins.

---

B: The supply chain is also continuing to expand toward end devices. Nvidia plans to invest $3.5 billion in MediaTek while extending its business into the global robotics market. SK Hynix, meanwhile, is considering building a factory in Japan to meet AI-driven demand for memory.

A: This shows that competition is no longer confined to cloud data centers. Phones, cars, robots, and edge devices are becoming the next major gateways for computing power.

B: Chinese robotics companies’ reliance on Nvidia’s chips and software also shows that beyond hardware, development tools and ecosystems create equally significant barriers to entry. Capital partnerships are further cementing these relationships.

---

A: Turning to the broader economy, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury has reached a 19-month high, inflation in Germany is picking up again, and India’s economic growth has once more exceeded expectations. Expectations of interest-rate hikes are rising across multiple markets.

B: Higher oil prices are adding further inflationary pressure. Crude prices climbed after clashes between the United States and Iran, prompting declines in the S&P 500 and global stock markets.

A: This matters especially for AI and technology assets. Higher risk-free rates reduce the present value of future profits, while rising financing costs force projects with enormous investment needs and unstable earnings to face cash-flow scrutiny sooner.

---

B: Energy supply chains are also being reshaped. After Iran-related conflict disrupted supplies, Asian refiners began purchasing more crude oil from Argentina. Meanwhile, a major refinery in the United Arab Emirates that had been affected by the fighting has returned to full production, providing some localized relief.

A: This is about more than oil prices simply rising or falling. Sources of supply are being reorganized, and refiners must rebalance shipping distances, crude compatibility, and supply security.

B: Another development worth watching is energy storage. As demand for battery storage grows, lithium miners are seeing a significant improvement in profits, with demand for lithium expanding beyond electric vehicles into power grids and broader energy systems.

---

A: Finally, let’s turn to regulation and security. Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey has warned the G20 that AI could pose a threat to the financial system. Sony and Warner, meanwhile, are suing Anthropic, alleging that it used songs to train its models without permission.

B: On top of that, the rise in AI-driven cyberattacks is putting greater pressure on security teams at hospitals and banks. A lending platform associated with Crypto.com has also suffered a hack involving roughly $74 million.

A: Together, these events show that the external costs of AI are becoming increasingly visible. Copyright licensing, training-data audits, financial risk controls, and investment in cybersecurity personnel could all shift from optional add-ons to fixed costs.

---

B: To sum up, AI remains at the center of capital and industrial expansion, but the market’s focus has shifted from “How quickly are capabilities improving?” to “Who bears the costs, who captures the profits, and who is responsible for the risks?”

A: Beyond AI, changes in interest rates, oil prices, exchange rates, and supply chains are determining how much it will cost companies to keep investing.
