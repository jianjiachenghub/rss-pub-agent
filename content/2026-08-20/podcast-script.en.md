A: Welcome to today’s Personal Daily Briefing. The main story worth watching today is that the AI race is shifting from “who has the strongest model” to “who controls the chips, computing power, access points, and costs.”

B: The other major theme comes from the financial markets: the more money poured into AI investment, the more investors focus on returns. Meanwhile, volatility in long-term U.S. Treasuries is increasing, making the market’s pricing benchmark less stable.

A: Let’s start with chips. Google has struck a $12 billion AI chip deal with Marvell. Although the timeline and supply arrangements have not been disclosed, the size of the deal alone shows that investment in custom chips has officially entered the tens-of-billions-of-dollars range.

B: More notably, Alphabet-owned Waymo is also developing custom chips for its robotaxis. In other words, custom computing is no longer just for large models—it is moving deeper into specific businesses such as autonomous driving. In the future, the difference between tech companies may be reflected not only in software, but also in their ability to design underlying hardware around their own use cases.

A: The second focus is Stripe’s agreement to acquire OpenRouter. OpenRouter connects different models, helping developers choose and switch between access channels, while Stripe specializes in payments, billing, and enterprise customer services. Combining the two effectively puts model routing, usage tracking, and settlement into a single system.

B: What this deal is really competing for is the infrastructure gateway to AI applications. Whoever controls the model-access and payment chain gains deeper insight into which models developers use and how much they spend. However, the value and specific terms of the deal have not yet been disclosed.

A: The model market itself is also changing. Reports indicate that Moonshot and ZAI are approaching the capabilities of OpenAI and Anthropic, while generating usable websites at roughly 75% lower cost than Claude.

B: This will encourage developers to choose models by task: more capable models for complex work, and lower-cost models for routine tasks. At the same time, OpenAI’s pledge not to retain customer data shows that enterprise competition has expanded beyond performance and pricing to privacy policies. The premium model providers once commanded through superior capabilities is being squeezed from both sides—by cost and data governance.

A: Now let’s look at the pressure to generate returns on AI spending. Alibaba raised quarterly capital expenditure to nearly $10 billion, while its profit fell more than 75% year over year during the same period. Ant Group’s profit growth has also stalled because of AI investment and expansion costs.

B: On the other side, Hudson River Trading has signed a multibillion-dollar, multiyear cloud services agreement with CoreWeave for trading research and model development. This shows that AI computing is shifting from a flexible research and development expense into a long-term infrastructure commitment. The question is changing as well: companies not only need enough money to buy computing power, but also need to prove that it can translate into revenue, efficiency, or competitive advantage.

A: The central issue in financial markets today is long-term U.S. Treasuries. Bessent tried to reassure the market, and the Treasury unexpectedly carried out a bond buyback, but the rally in 30-year Treasuries failed to hold. Investors remain concerned about whether long-term supply and debt-management strategies are predictable enough.

B: The market is demanding greater compensation for duration risk, and demand for Treasuries has become more sensitive to yield levels. Simply put, investors are no longer willing to absorb new debt unconditionally. Interestingly, in this environment, higher-risk bank AT1 bonds have remained relatively stable, creating a temporary inversion in the bond market’s traditional risk hierarchy.

A: Regulation and geopolitics cannot be ignored either. Massachusetts has proposed strict AI safety safeguards, prompting a public disagreement between Anthropic and OpenAI. Meanwhile, U.S.-Canada trade negotiations have raised concerns in Canada that its domestic technology and AI rules could become bargaining chips.

B: This means AI regulation is no longer just a matter of technical safety. It also involves competition over rules between states and the federal government, companies and governments, and different countries. At the same time, U.S. talk of “economic warfare” against Iran, along with the risk that conflict could drive up energy prices, has led Sweden’s central bank to keep the possibility of an interest-rate hike later this year on the table.

A: Finally, let’s look at market performance. Rising oil prices and bond yields weighed on U.S. stocks, with Walmart shares falling sharply. But as yields eased during parts of the session and expectations of greater liquidity provided support, Bitcoin climbed back above $70,000. In China, Shanghai continued to ease property policies, while AI-related capital focused more heavily on hard-tech assets such as the STAR 50 Index.

B: So today’s developments can be summed up in one sentence: the AI race is becoming fully capitalized, while interest rates, regulation, and geopolitical risks will determine what those investments are ultimately worth. For investors and businesses, the next thing to watch is not just technological progress, but whether costs, cash flow, and rules can all align.
